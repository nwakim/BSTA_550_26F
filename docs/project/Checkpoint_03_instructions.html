Goal

NoteScenarios A and B: from the mean and SD to the shape and rate

The papers give a mean and an SD, but pgamma() and rgamma() need the shape \(r\) and rate \(\lambda\). From Lesson 13, a \(\text{Gamma}(r, \lambda)\) random variable has

\[\text{E}(Y) = \frac{r}{\lambda} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \text{Var}(Y) = \frac{r}{\lambda^2}.\]

Set these equal to your values (\(\text{E}(Y)\) = mean and \(\text{Var}(Y) = \text{SD}^2\)) and solve for \(r\) and \(\lambda\):

  1. Find the rate. Divide the mean by the variance. The \(r\)’s cancel: \[\frac{\text{E}(Y)}{\text{Var}(Y)} = \frac{r/\lambda}{r/\lambda^2} = \lambda, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \lambda = \frac{\text{mean}}{\text{SD}^2}.\]
  2. Find the shape. Rearrange \(\text{E}(Y) = r/\lambda\) to get \(r = \lambda \cdot \text{mean}\). Plugging in \(\lambda\) from Step 1: \[r = \frac{\text{mean}}{\text{SD}^2} \cdot \text{mean} = \left(\frac{\text{mean}}{\text{SD}}\right)^2.\]
  3. Check. Plug \(r\) and \(\lambda\) back in: \(r/\lambda\) should give your mean, and \(\sqrt{r/\lambda^2}\) should give your SD.

Example (Scenario A): with mean = 14 mm and SD = 9 mm,

\[\lambda = \frac{14}{9^2} = \frac{14}{81} \approx 0.173 \text{ per mm}, \qquad r = \left(\frac{14}{9}\right)^2 \approx 2.42.\]

Check: \(r/\lambda = 2.42 / 0.173 \approx 14\) mm and \(\sqrt{2.42/0.173^2} \approx 9\) mm. Scenario B: follow the same steps with your mean and SD from Del Águila-Mejía et al. (2022). In R, you can save each step:

gamma_mean <- 14
gamma_sd <- 9

gamma_rate <- gamma_mean / gamma_sd^2
gamma_shape <- gamma_rate * gamma_mean

c(shape = gamma_shape, rate = gamma_rate)

Notice that the shape \(r\) does not have to be a whole number (see the Remarks slide in Lesson 13).

4. Simulate averages and discover the Central Limit Theorem

Let \(\bar{Y}_n\) be the average of \(n\) independent observations of \(Y\). In this task, you will use simulation to see how \(\bar{Y}_n\) behaves.

  • For \(n = 5\), \(30\), and \(100\) (and also your scenario’s \(n\) if it is not one of these, such as \(n = 50\) for Scenario B), simulate at least 10,000 sample means.
  • For each \(n\), make a histogram of your sample means, with the Normal curve from the callout below overlaid.
  • Compare the mean and SD of your simulated sample means with the values in the callout below.
  • In 2–3 sentences, describe what happens to the distribution of \(\bar{Y}_n\) as \(n\) gets bigger. How quickly does it look Normal, and how does the shape (skewness) of \(Y\) affect this?
NoteWhat to look for: the Central Limit Theorem (a preview of Lesson 17)

If \(Y\) has mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\), then for large enough \(n\), the average of \(n\) independent observations is approximately Normal:

\[\bar{Y}_n \approx N\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)\]

So the sample means are centered at the same mean \(\mu\) as one observation, but their SD is only \(\sigma / \sqrt{n}\): averages vary much less than single values. Your simulation will let you check this before we prove it in class.

5. Answer your guiding question

6. Stretch (optional, +1 bonus)

How good is the CLT approximation for your scenario?

  • Scenarios A and B: The average of \(n\) independent Gamma(shape \(r\), rate \(\lambda\)) random variables has exactly a Gamma distribution with shape \(n \cdot r\) and rate \(n \cdot \lambda\) (we will see why with moment generating functions in Lesson 18). Use pgamma() to find the exact probability for your guiding question, and compare it with your CLT approximation and your simulation. Then try \(n = 5\): is the CLT approximation still close?
  • Scenario C: Your \(Y\) is Normal, so the average of \(n\) babies is exactly Normal for every \(n\). Check this with your simulation: for \(n = 5\), compare the simulated \(P(\bar{Y}_5 < 3{,}300)\) with the pnorm() answer, and explain why they agree even though \(n\) is small.
