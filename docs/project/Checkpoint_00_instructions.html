Checkpoint 0: Choose Your Scenario

Modified

September 30, 2026

Important

Due 10/11 (4 points). See Project Central for the full project overview.

Goal

Choose the public health scenario you will follow all quarter, read the published sources that come with it, and pull out the numbers you will use in Checkpoints 1–3.

You do not need to search for sources on your own. Each scenario below comes with a short source packet: the setting is fixed, and the papers you need are listed for you. Your job in this checkpoint is to read those sources, find the numbers, and describe them in your own words.

How the scenarios work

Each scenario card below has:

  • The setting: who the population is and what happens to them. Please stay inside this setting all quarter.
  • The source packet: 1–3 published sources. Every number you use must come from these sources or from a value the card gives you.
  • Course values: a few numbers that the papers do not report in a usable form. The card gives you these directly. Use them as written.
NoteWords you might see in the papers

The papers use some terms we have not covered yet. You do not need to understand them fully for Checkpoint 0. Use this list to find the right number, and we will build each idea in class.

Term in the paper What it means in plain words Checkpoint used in
Prevalence Out of everyone in the group, the fraction who have the condition CP 1
Sensitivity Out of the people who have the condition, the fraction who test positive CP 1
Specificity Out of the people who do not have the condition, the fraction who test negative CP 1
False-positive rate Out of the people who do not have the condition, the fraction who test positive (1 − specificity) CP 1
Positive predictive value (PPV) Out of the people who test positive, the fraction who have the condition. You will calculate this in CP 1, so do not record it now. CP 1
Abnormal interpretation rate (AIR) Out of all screening exams, the fraction read as abnormal (the woman is called back) CP 2
Cancer detection rate (CDR) The number of cancers the screen found, per 1,000 exams. This is not the prevalence, because it leaves out the cancers the screen missed. —
Secondary attack rate (SAR) Out of the people exposed to a case (e.g., the people they live with), the fraction who get infected CP 2
“Per 1,000” or “per 10,000” A count out of 1,000 or 10,000 people. To turn it into a decimal, divide: 3 per 10,000 = 3 / 10,000 = 0.0003. CP 1, CP 2
Mean The average value CP 3
Median and interquartile range (IQR) The middle value, and the range that holds the middle half of the values CP 3
Standard deviation (SD) How spread out the values are around the mean CP 3
95% confidence interval (CI) A range of plausible values around an estimate. Record only the main estimate, not the interval. —

Community breast cancer screening program

The setting: A county health department runs a mobile 3D mammography van that brings free breast cancer screening to rural and underserved communities. The program screens women ages 40–74, following the current U.S. recommendation. A radiologist reads each exam as either normal or abnormal, and every woman with an abnormal read needs a follow-up diagnostic appointment. The program manager needs to know what an abnormal read means for the women served, how many follow-up appointments to reserve after each van day, and whether screening is finding cancers while they are still small.

Source packet

  1. Lee CI, Abraham L, Miglioretti DL, et al. National performance benchmarks for screening digital breast tomosynthesis: Update from the Breast Cancer Surveillance Consortium. Radiology. 2023;307(4):e222499. Free full text

This one paper has everything you need. Look in the Abstract and the Results section. Use the numbers for DBT (digital breast tomosynthesis), not for digital mammography (DM).

What each checkpoint will look at

Checkpoint What we model Where it comes from
CP 1 The chance a woman has cancer, given an abnormal read Sensitivity and specificity (paper); prevalence (course value below)
CP 2 The number of abnormal reads (follow-up appointments needed) out of 40 screens on one van day Abnormal interpretation rate (paper)
CP 3 The size of a tumor found by screening, in millimeters Median and IQR (paper); mean and SD (course values below)

Course values

  • Prevalence: 6.6 cancers per 1,000 women screened. (The paper reports the cancer detection rate, which only counts the cancers that the screen found. We divided it by the sensitivity to include the cancers the screen missed.)
  • Screens per van day: 40.
  • Tumor size: mean of about 14 mm, SD of about 9 mm. (The paper reports a median and IQR. These values match that median and IQR for a right-skewed shape.)

CP 1: sensitivity “Sensitivity” (DBT)
CP 1: specificity “Specificity” (DBT)

CP 3: mean and SD Course values on this card. The paper reports a median and IQR for tumor size, which you may list too.

The abstract reports each measure for DBT and for DM side by side, so double-check that you are copying the DBT number.

County COVID-19 testing and contact tracing

The setting: A county health department gives out free rapid at-home antigen tests so that residents without symptoms can screen themselves, for example before visiting an older relative. When a resident tests positive and is confirmed as a case, the department’s contact tracers follow up with the people they live with. The department needs to know how much to trust a positive screening test, how many household members of each case are likely to be infected (to plan test kits and isolation support), and how long household contacts should watch for symptoms.

Source packet

  1. Cochrane. How accurate are rapid antigen tests for diagnosing COVID-19? Plain language summary of Dinnes J, et al. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2025 update (CD013705). Summary page · Full review
  2. Madewell ZJ, Yang Y, Longini IM Jr, Halloran ME, Dean NE. Household secondary attack rates of SARS-CoV-2 by variant and vaccination status: An updated systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA Network Open. 2022;5(4):e229317. Free full text
  3. Wu Y, Kang L, Guo Z, Liu J, Liu M, Liang W. Incubation period of COVID-19 caused by unique SARS-CoV-2 strains: A systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA Network Open. 2022;5(8):e2228008. Free full text

For source 1, use the overall numbers for people without symptoms. For sources 2 and 3, use the numbers for the Omicron variant. Look in the Abstract or Key Points box of each paper.

What each checkpoint will look at

Checkpoint What we model Where it comes from
CP 1 The chance a resident is infected, given a positive rapid test Sensitivity and specificity (source 1); prevalence (course value below)
CP 2 The number of household contacts of one case who get infected Household secondary attack rate for Omicron (source 2)
CP 3 The incubation period: days from infection until symptoms start Mean incubation period for Omicron (source 3)

Course values

  • Prevalence: 0.5% (5 out of every 1,000 residents without symptoms who test). This is the example prevalence the Cochrane summary uses for general screening.
  • Household contacts per case: 3 (a household of 4 people, with 1 case).

CP 1: sensitivity “Average sensitivity” (source 1). Use the average across all evaluations, not the numbers for subgroups such as “general screening” or “suspected exposure”.
CP 1: specificity “Average specificity” (source 1), also across all evaluations
CP 2: chance of a “yes” “Household secondary attack rate” or “household SAR” for Omicron (source 2). A “yes” is a household contact who gets infected.
CP 2: group size Course value on this card
CP 3: mean Mean incubation period for Omicron (source 3)
CP 3: SD Source 3 does not report an SD, so write “not reported”. Do not use the range of incubation periods as the SD.

For source 1, use the numbers on the linked 2025 summary page. Older versions of this review report different numbers.

State newborn heart screening program

The setting: A state health department runs a newborn screening program that requires every baby born in the state to get pulse oximetry screening before going home. Pulse oximetry is a painless test that measures the oxygen level in the blood. It looks for critical congenital heart defects (CCHD), which are serious heart problems that need treatment soon after birth. The state has about 40,000 births a year. The program needs to know what a positive screen means for families, how many babies with CCHD to expect each year (to plan follow-up with pediatric heart specialists), and what typical birth weight looks like across the state’s newborns.

Source packet

  1. Cochrane. Pulse oximetry for diagnosis of critical congenital heart defects. Plain language summary of Plana MN, Zamora J, Suresh G, et al. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. 2018;3:CD011912. Summary page · Full review
  2. Donahue SMA, Kleinman KP, Gillman MW, Oken E. Trends in birth weight and gestational length among singleton term births in the United States: 1990–2005. Obstetrics & Gynecology. 2010;115(2 Pt 1):357–364. Free full text

For source 1, look for the numbers “per 10,000 newborns” and the false-positive rate. Use the false-positive rate instead of the specificity, because the specificity (99.9%) is rounded too much to be useful. For source 2, use the mean and SD for 2005.

What each checkpoint will look at

Checkpoint What we model Where it comes from
CP 1 The chance a baby has CCHD, given a positive screen Prevalence, sensitivity, and false-positive rate (source 1)
CP 2 The number of babies born with CCHD in the state in one year Prevalence (source 1); births per year (course value below)
CP 3 Birth weight, in grams Mean and SD for 2005 (source 2)

Course values

  • Births per year in the state: 40,000.
TipFinding your numbers (Scenario C)
Row in your Checkpoint 0 table What it is called in the source

CP 1: sensitivity “Sensitivity” (source 1)
CP 1: false-positive rate “False-positive rate” (source 1). Write it as \(P(T^+ \mid D^c)\), not as a specificity.

CP 2: group size Course value on this card

Tasks

1. Team and scenario

  • State whether you are working alone or with a partner (and your partner’s name).
  • State which scenario you chose (A, B, or C).
  • In 3–5 sentences, and in your own words, describe the scenario: Who is the population? What is being tested, counted, or measured? What decision does the public health program need to make, and why does it matter for the population it serves?

2. Find your numbers in the sources

Fill in the table below for your scenario. For each row:

  • Value: Write the number from the source (or the course value from your scenario card).
  • Where I found it: Name the source (1, 2, or 3) and the section, table, or sentence where the number appears (for example, “Source 1, Abstract, Results”). For a number from your scenario card, write “course value”.
  • The Finding your numbers box on your scenario card tells you what each number is called in the source.
  • Probability statement: For the three Checkpoint 1 rows only, write the value as a probability. Define your events first, for example, \(D\) = has the condition and \(T^+\) = tests positive. Then write, for example, \(P(T^+ \mid D) = 0.874\).

1 Out of everyone screened or tested, the fraction who have the condition
1 Out of the people who have the condition, the fraction who test positive
1 Out of the people who do not have the condition, the fraction who test negative (A and B) or test positive (C)

WarningRecord only what the source says
  • Copy the main estimate only. Do not include the 95% confidence interval.
  • Do not calculate anything new yet. For example, do not calculate the chance that someone with a positive test has the condition. That is the job of Checkpoint 1.
  • Convert percents and “per 1,000” or “per 10,000” values to decimals in the probability statement column (e.g., 87.4% becomes 0.874, and 3 per 10,000 becomes 0.0003).

3. Your guiding questions

Write one question for each checkpoint that your analysis will answer. You may use the examples below for your scenario, or change them (for example, a different cutoff).

Scenario A (community breast cancer screening program)

  • CP 1: If a woman screened on the van gets an abnormal read, what is the probability that she has breast cancer?
  • CP 2: If the program reserves 6 follow-up appointments after each van day of 40 screens, what is the probability that it needs more than 6?
  • CP 3: What is the probability that the average size of the next 30 tumors the program finds is more than 15 mm?

Scenario B (county COVID-19 testing and contact tracing)

  • CP 1: If a resident without symptoms gets a positive rapid test, what is the probability that they are infected?
  • CP 2: For a case who lives with 3 other people, what is the probability that at least 2 of them also get infected?
  • CP 3: What is the probability that the average incubation period across the county’s next 50 cases is more than 4 days?

Scenario C (state newborn heart screening program)

  • CP 1: If a newborn has a positive pulse oximetry screen, what is the probability that the baby has CCHD?
  • CP 2: With 40,000 births a year, what is the probability that the state has 30 or more babies with CCHD in one year?
  • CP 3: What is the probability that the average birth weight of 100 babies born in one county in a month is less than 3,300 grams?

These can change later. This step is to make sure your scenario works for all three checkpoints.
