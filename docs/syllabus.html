Description

Welcome to BSTA 550! This course introduces the methods and concepts of probability theory: conditional probability and independence, combinatorics, discrete and continuous random variables, probability distributions, joint distributions, expectation, transformations of random variables, and the central limit theorem. You’ll apply these concepts and build statistical reasoning through simulations in R.

Course Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, you should be able to…

  1. Assign probability to a chance event using concepts of probability (including fundamental axioms, properties, and counting).
  2. Compute probabilities for discrete random variables (including random variables following Bernoulli, binomial, geometric, and Poisson distributions).
  3. Compute probabilities for continuous random variables (including random variables following Normal, Gamma, and Beta distributions).
  4. Perform statistical computations and simulations using R.

How each activity is graded

Homework (30%)

Everyone has a different amount of time for coursework, and homework is for learning, so grading is simple: you get a check if you complete at least 75% of the question parts, whether your answers are right or wrong. Each of the 9 homework assignments counts equally, and the TAs grade them for completeness.

  • On time: you get the check and feedback on one or more problems.
  • Late: you can turn in homework any time before the course ends with no grade penalty, but you won’t get feedback. Compare your work to the solutions or bring it to office hours.

Homework is the most helpful work in this class, so I encourage you to keep up with it and put in what effort you can!

Quizzes (24%)

There are 3 quizzes, each worth 8%. They’re graded for correctness, with partial credit for showing your work and reasoning. Quizzes are open book but must be done independently, and AI is not allowed (see the Generative AI Policy). You’ll get solutions and feedback afterward, so use them to fill in any gaps before the next quiz.

Project Checkpoints (32%)

There are 4 checkpoints, each worth an equal share of the 32%. Each one is graded with a rubric that focuses on accurate calculations and clear explanations of your reasoning and background. You’ll get feedback on each checkpoint, and I expect you to use it in your next checkpoint and your final brief. You get one no-questions-asked, 3-day extension for a checkpoint (see the late policy below).

Project Final Brief (14%)

The final brief brings together your checkpoint work into a summary of your scenario. It’s graded with a rubric that looks at accurate results, clear context for your scenario, and communicating your findings to your audience. Because it’s due at the end of the quarter, feedback will be lighter, but feel free to email me with questions about your grade.

Generative AI Policy

This policy is adapted from the OHSU-PSU SPH Biostatistics program’s AI policy, which classifies BSTA 550 as a foundational course. Everything you need for this class is below.

Using AI is not required in this class. If you choose to use it, please follow these guidelines. AI tools (like ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Gemini, or an AI assistant built into RStudio) are becoming an essential skill for biostatisticians, so the goal is to use them responsibly, not avoid them.

The toolbox analogy

Think of this class as assembling a toolbox. A new handyperson might need help finding their tools or remembering which one does what, but eventually using them becomes second nature.

For now, generative AI can help us find and use our tools, but we’re working toward using them as second nature!

Four guiding principles

  1. Learning comes first. Ask yourself: Is AI helping me learn this skill, or doing the skill for me? If it’s doing the skill the assignment is meant to teach, don’t use it that way.
  2. Understanding is the priority. You should be able to explain, reproduce, and defend (without AI) any code, calculation, or conclusion you turn in. Following along with an AI answer is not the same as understanding it!
  3. Build the foundation first. In this course, working through probability calculations and writing R code is the skill we’re practicing. That’s why some AI uses are limited here, not because AI is untrustworthy.
  4. Be honest about AI use. Tell me when and how you used AI (see the AI Use Statement below).

There may be exceptions. For example, if a question asks you to explain what a function does, you can’t paste in AI’s explanation. When in doubt, just ask me!

Never allowed

  • Copying and pasting AI-generated answers into your assignment
  • Pasting all or part of an assignment into AI and asking for the solution
  • Presenting AI output as your own reasoning (e.g., asking AI what to conclude and writing it as your own conclusion)
  • Using AI during a quiz
  • Uploading confidential or restricted data (including any data with protected health information) into an AI tool, unless it’s a university-approved platform under a data use agreement
  • Using AI-generated datasets or output as if they came from the assigned data and methods
  • Using AI-generated references, results, or facts without verifying them yourself
  • Submitting AI-assisted work without an AI Use Statement

Even for allowed uses, always inspect AI-generated code and its output, whether it’s a whole chunk or a single comma. You’re responsible for making sure it does what it’s supposed to.

Homework is graded on completion, so I’d much rather see a half-written answer that shows your thinking than a polished answer from AI. And if AI helped you understand something, tell me! I’d love to incorporate it into future classes.

AI Use Statement

If you used AI on an assignment, add a brief statement at the top that includes which tool(s) you used, what for (a sentence or two), and where it contributed to any written interpretations. For example:

AI Use Statement: Claude was used to explain an error message in Q3 and to give grammar feedback on my final brief. All statistical reasoning, code review, and conclusions are my own.

You don’t need to share a link or transcript of your conversation.

If the guidelines aren’t followed

If I or a TA notice AI was used outside these guidelines, we’ll reach out to let you know which assignment and which guideline, and you can choose to:

  1. Take a 0 on the assignment as submitted,
  2. Redo the assignment without AI for a new score, or
  3. (Code and hand calculations only) Explain to me (by email or in a meeting) exactly how you used AI and show that you can do the work on your own. If that works out, you keep your original grade; if not, the question(s) where AI was used get 0 points.

First-time, good-faith mistakes (like forgetting an AI Use Statement) will generally be treated as a learning moment, not an integrity issue. Undisclosed AI use, using AI on a quiz, or turning in work you can’t explain may lead to consequences under this policy and university academic conduct policies.

Academic Integrity

As a PSU/OHSU student, you’re expected to act with professionalism and integrity. Academic misconduct (plagiarism, cheating, fabricating data, falsifying records, or helping others do so) will be taken seriously. Quizzes must reflect your own individual effort, and any resources you use should be cited.
