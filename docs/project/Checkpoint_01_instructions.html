Checkpoint 1: When Is a Positive Really Positive?

Modified

October 7, 2026

Important

Due 10/18 (12 points). See Project Central for the full project overview. Make sure you have addressed any feedback from Checkpoint 0.

Goal

Use conditional probability and Bayes’ rule to answer: If someone served by my public health program gets a positive result, how likely is it that they truly have the condition?

Course topics used: events, conditional probability, the Law of Total Probability, Bayes’ rule, independence.

Your scenario

Use the input values from your Checkpoint 0 table, with any corrections from my feedback. Every value should come from your source packet or from a course value on your scenario card.

  • Scenario B

Community breast cancer screening program

  • Events: \(D\) = has breast cancer; \(T^+\) = the mammogram is read as abnormal.
  • Inputs: prevalence (course value), sensitivity, and specificity from Lee et al. (2023).
  • Program question: What does an abnormal read mean for a woman screened on the van?

County COVID-19 testing and contact tracing

  • Events: \(D\) = infected with SARS-CoV-2; \(T^+\) = the rapid antigen test is positive.
  • Inputs: prevalence (course value), and sensitivity and specificity for people without symptoms from the Cochrane review.
  • Program question: How much should a resident without symptoms trust a positive rapid test?

State newborn heart screening program

  • Events: \(D\) = has a critical congenital heart defect (CCHD); \(T^+\) = the pulse oximetry screen is positive.
  • Inputs: prevalence, sensitivity, and false-positive rate from the Cochrane review. Your specificity is \(1 - (\text{false-positive rate})\).
  • Program question: What does a positive screen mean for a newborn’s family?

Tasks

1. Set up the problem

  • Define your events with notation, using the events for your scenario above.
  • Write each input value as a probability statement, for example \(P(T^+ \mid D) = 0.874\). Cite the source for each value.
  • Draw a tree diagram or a 2×2 table for a hypothetical population of 100,000 people screened by your program.

2. Solve it analytically

  • Use the Law of Total Probability to find \(P(T^+)\).
  • Use Bayes’ rule to find the positive predictive value \(P(D \mid T^+)\) and the negative predictive value \(P(D^c \mid T^-)\).
  • Show your work. You may use R as a calculator, but write out each formula first.

3. Simulate it

  • Simulate a population of at least 100,000 people in R. Assign each person a disease status, then a test result that depends on their disease status.
  • Estimate PPV and NPV from your simulated population and compare them with your analytic answers.
  • In 1–2 sentences, explain why the simulated and analytic values are close but not identical.

Starter code (replace the placeholder input values with your own):

set.seed(550)
N    <- 1e5
prev <- 0.01   # P(D): replace with your value
sens <- 0.90   # P(T+ | D): replace with your value
spec <- 0.95   # P(T- | D^c): replace with your value

disease  <- rbinom(N, size = 1, prob = prev)
test_pos <- ifelse(disease == 1,
                   rbinom(N, size = 1, prob = sens),
                   rbinom(N, size = 1, prob = 1 - spec))

ppv_sim   <- mean(disease[test_pos == 1])
ppv_bayes <- (sens * prev) / (sens * prev + (1 - spec) * (1 - prev))

c(simulated = ppv_sim, analytic = ppv_bayes)
TipRare conditions need big populations

If your prevalence is very small (Scenario C has about 6 cases per 10,000 babies), a population of 100,000 has only a few dozen true cases, so your simulated PPV will bounce around. Try \(N = 10^6\) and see whether your simulated values get closer to your analytic answers.

4. Explore: how does prevalence matter?

  • Compute the analytic PPV for prevalence values from 0.1% to 50%, keeping sensitivity and specificity fixed.
  • Plot PPV (y-axis) against prevalence (x-axis), and mark your scenario’s prevalence on the plot.
  • In 2–3 sentences, describe what happens to PPV as prevalence changes. Then describe a group or setting your program might serve where the prevalence would be higher or lower than your scenario’s value (for example, women with a family history of breast cancer, residents tested during a COVID-19 surge, or babies whose prenatal ultrasound showed a possible heart problem). How would a positive result mean something different there?

5. Say it

Your program sends a letter or text message to every person with a positive result. Write a short paragraph (4–6 sentences) for that message. Explain what the result means using natural frequencies (e.g., “Out of 1,000 people who get a positive result, about ___ actually have…”) rather than probability notation, and say what the next step is (a follow-up appointment, a confirmatory test, or a heart ultrasound).
