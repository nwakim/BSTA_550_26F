Final Project: Public Health Brief

Important

Goal

All quarter, you have used probability and simulation to study one public health program. In the final project, you will pull that work together and translate it for the person who runs the program: a program manager who needs to make decisions but has never taken a probability class and will never see your code.

Your brief should answer one question: What does probability tell us about this program, and what should the manager keep in mind when making decisions?

Your three guiding questions

You already wrote the questions your brief will answer. Your three guiding questions from Checkpoint 0 (with any changes from my feedback) are the manager’s questions, and each finding in your brief answers one of them:

Finding Checkpoint Your guiding question asked… Example (Scenario B)
1. What a positive result means CP 1 what a positive result means for one person If a resident without symptoms gets a positive rapid test, what is the probability that they are infected?
2. Planning for the count CP 2 how often an unusual day, household, or year happens For a case who lives with 3 other people, what is the probability that at least 2 of them also get infected?
3. One person versus the average CP 3 how often an average across many people is beyond a cutoff What is the probability that the average incubation period across the county’s next 50 cases is more than 3.5 days?

You also already wrote a first draft of each finding: the “Say it” task at the end of each checkpoint. Start from those.

The public health brief

Who you are writing for

Your reader is the manager of the program in your scenario:

  • Scenario A: the manager of the county’s mobile mammography van program.
  • Scenario B: the manager of the county health department’s COVID-19 testing and contact tracing team.
  • Scenario C: the manager of the state’s newborn screening program.

This person knows their program well. They know what a mammogram, a rapid test, or a pulse oximetry screen is. They do not know what \(P(D \mid T^+)\), a Binomial distribution, or the Central Limit Theorem is, and they do not need to. They need to know what your results mean for the decisions they make.

Format

Make your brief with the brief template, which builds a one-page information page with colored boxes for each section and a tab for each finding.

You can download the brief template here. The link takes you to GitHub, where you can click the download button (the arrow pointing down) near the top right of the file. Rename it lastname_firstinitial_final_brief.qmd.

  • Length: at most 600 words on the page, not counting the “How we got these numbers” box, text inside your plots, the sources, or the AI Use Statement. The “How we got these numbers” box can have at most 120 words.
  • Style: You are welcome to change the colors and theme (the template shows you where), as long as every section is there and the page is easy to read.

What your brief should include

The template has a box for each of these sections, in this order:

  1. Title and the bottom line
  2. The program and the question
  3. Where the numbers come from
  4. How we got these numbers
  5. What we found: Finding 1, Finding 2, and Finding 3, one tab each
  6. What to keep in mind
  7. Sources and AI Use Statement, in small print at the bottom

Title and the bottom line

  • Title: Keep it plain and descriptive. A reader should know what the brief is about before they read a full sentence. For example: “What Probability Tells Us About Our COVID-19 Testing and Contact Tracing Program.” Put your name(s) in the author line.
  • The bottom line: One sentence, in plain language, that states the most important thing you want the manager to remember. If the manager only reads one line, this is it.
Tip

Write the title and the bottom line last, after you have written your findings. It is much easier to summarize findings you have already written.

The program and the question

In 2–4 sentences, describe the program (who it serves, and what test, count, or measurement it deals with) and the decisions the manager has to make. You can start from the scenario description you wrote in Checkpoint 0.

Where the numbers come from

In 2–3 sentences, explain in plain language where your input values come from: the published studies in your source packet and the course values on your scenario card. You do not need to list every number here. The point is to show the reader that your results rest on real, published estimates.

How we got these numbers

In at most 120 words, explain in plain language how you turned those numbers into your findings: one sentence for each finding, and one sentence about how you checked your results with simulation. Use no notation, formulas, or distribution names. For example, for Scenario B:

  • Finding 1: We imagined 10,000 residents without symptoms taking a rapid test and used the published accuracy of rapid tests to count how many people with a positive result are actually infected.
  • Finding 2: We treated each of the 3 people who live with a case as their own yes-or-no outcome with the same chance of infection, and worked out how often at least 2 of them get infected.
  • Finding 3: We used the published average and spread of the time from infection to symptoms to describe one person, then worked out how much the average across 50 cases varies.
  • Checking our work: We checked every result by having a computer simulate thousands of residents, households, and groups of cases.

What we found

You will have three findings, one from each checkpoint, each in its own tab. Each finding has three parts:

  • A short headline in the tab name: the finding number and a few words that name the point, like a newspaper subheading. For example: “Finding 1: Most positive rapid tests are false alarms.”
  • One visual: a polished plot or table from your checkpoint (see Polishing your visuals).
  • A 2–4 sentence caption: state the key number, then say what it means for the program.

Here is what each finding should cover, and where to start:

Finding Start from What it should tell the manager Visual
1. What a positive result means CP 1 “Say it” message and Task 4 Out of the people who get a positive result, how many actually have the condition? How would this change in a group with a higher or lower prevalence? A natural frequency table or bar chart (e.g., “out of 1,000 people screened…”), or your PPV-versus-prevalence plot
2. Planning for the count CP 2 “Say it” paragraph What is a typical day, household, or year? How often does the situation in your guiding question happen, and what does that mean for planning? Your PMF plot, without the simulation, with the bars past your cutoff highlighted
3. One person versus the average CP 3 “Say it” paragraph How different is what happens to one person from what happens on average across many people? The distribution for one person and the distribution of the average, on the same axis

The code chunk in each tab of the template has a hint for making these visuals.

Use natural frequencies, with the probability in parentheses if it helps, just like in your Checkpoint 2 and 3 “Say it” paragraphs. For example, a Finding 1 caption might read:

Finding 1: Most positive rapid tests are false alarms

Out of every 10,000 residents without symptoms who take a rapid test, about 77 will test positive, but only about 28 of those 77 are actually infected (probability 0.36). This means that a positive rapid test in someone without symptoms should be confirmed with a PCR test before we ask them and their household to isolate.

NoteBe honest about uncertainty

Your numbers come from models built on published estimates, not from the program’s own data. Use words like “about,” “we expect,” and “roughly” rather than stating results as exact facts. For example, “we expect about 1 of the 3 people who live with each case to get infected,” not “1 of the 3 people will get infected.”

What to keep in mind

In 3–5 sentences (or bullet points), tell the manager what your findings mean for their decisions. For example: how to talk with people who get a positive result, how much capacity to plan for, or how to read the averages the program reports.

Include at least one assumption from your checkpoints that might not hold in real life, and explain in plain language how it could change your advice. Good places to look:

  • the assumption most likely to fail from Checkpoint 2 (and your Stretch result, if you did it),
  • where your distribution might not match the real quantity, from Checkpoint 3, Task 2.
Tip

You do not need to make formal policy recommendations. Describe what your results suggest and what is worth a closer look. Be careful not to claim more than your models can support.

Sources and AI Use Statement

At the bottom of the page, in small print:

  • Sources: full citations for the published sources you used from your source packet (authors, title, journal, year, and a link).
  • AI Use Statement: say whether you used AI, and if so, how. If not, write “AI was not used in this assignment.”

Polishing your visuals

The plots from your checkpoints were made for me. In the brief, they need to stand on their own for a manager. Before you add each one, revise it so that it has:

  • A plain-language, descriptive title (e.g., “Household members infected per COVID-19 case (homes of 4),” not “PMF of X”)
  • Axis labels in plain language, with units (no \(X\), \(Y\), or variable names from your code)
  • Only the information the reader needs. Remove simulation-versus-theory comparisons, extra lines, and default legends that do not help the reader.
  • A deliberate color choice that is consistent across your visuals (for example, the same colors as your page’s boxes), and a clean theme (e.g., theme_minimal() instead of the default gray background)
  • Text that is large enough to read on the page (e.g., theme_minimal(base_size = 16))

The template sets the size of every plot. To change the size of one plot, add #| fig-width: 7 and #| fig-height: 4 (in inches) at the top of its code chunk.

Tip

Simpler is better. A clear bar chart with one highlighted bar is often easier to understand than a detailed plot. Ask yourself: what is the one thing I want the manager to see in this visual?

Before you submit

Read your brief as if you were the program manager seeing it for the first time:

  • Does it read cleanly from top to bottom?
  • Does every finding have a headline, a visual, and a caption?
  • Is there anything left that still sounds like it was written for this class instead of for the manager (probability notation, distribution names, variable names, or numbers without an explanation)?
  • Is there any leftover template text in [square brackets]?
  • Are all of your numbers the corrected values, after addressing my feedback on each checkpoint?
  • Is the page within 600 words (and the “How we got these numbers” box within 120)?
